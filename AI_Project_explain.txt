# 4. EDA — Exploratory Data Analysis

This sounds complicated, but the idea is extremely simple.

Imagine your teacher gives you a box containing 1,000 newspapers.

Before reading all of them, you start asking:

- How many newspapers are there?
- Which newspaper has the longest headline?
- Which words appear most often?
- Are there missing headlines?
- Are some newspapers repeated?

You are exploring the collection to understand what is inside.

That is EDA.

Simplest definition: EDA means examining and summarising data to understand it before building the AI model.

EDA stands for Exploratory Data Analysis.

### What exactly will we explore?

Our Project 19 guidelines specifically mention three things:

A. Headline length

Some headlines are short:

Why?

To understand the structure of our data.

We might discover that words such as:

- Government
- President
- Election
- Pakistan
- Report

appear very frequently.

We can count these words and show the most common ones in a chart.

Why?

To understand what our headlines generally discuss.

If the dataset contains a source column, we can examine which sources appear most frequently.

For example, we might find that one source contributes 2,000 headlines while another contributes only 100.

Why?

To understand where our examples came from and identify possible imbalance or bias.

# 6. TF-IDF — How does a computer understand words?

[Model Input and Output Structure in Machine Learning | by Chandan Pal | Feb, 2026 | Medium](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/QflgKqFq5T-yi8kCdhAgCT6z9dTm3FRC4ubJVYRqYSzEWLWLgxU3sal7SvR6muTxkvCU1jfHZgAu4TWFpta82Az1557gNiut9hLWBuZE60zpxKuhepcm4RTxtMYr5_b8L_iX0Cpa3QGkFcDEhcW-gLfZ5ZgW39zFOnRIeeK291s?purpose=inline)

[medium.com](https://medium.com/%40techie_chandan/model-input-and-output-structure-in-machine-learning-explained-df4a12b81931?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is one of the most important ideas in our project.

Imagine I tell you:

"Ali is a very good student."

You understand the sentence because you know English.

But a computer model cannot directly understand words in the same way a human does. We need to represent the words using numbers.

Let's use a simple example.

Suppose we have three headlines:

1. Government announces new policy.
2. Government announces new school.
3. Scientists discover new planet.

We can create a table showing whether a word appears in each headline.

| Headline | Government | Announces | New | School | Scientists | Planet |
| -------- | ---------- | --------- | --- | ------ | ---------- | ------ |
| 1        | 1          | 1         | 1   | 0      | 0          | 0      |
| 2        | 1          | 1         | 1   | 1      | 0          | 0      |
| 3        | 0          | 0         | 1   | 0      | 1          | 1      |

Here:

- `1` means the word is present.
- `0` means the word is absent.

Now the computer has a numerical representation of the headlines!

But there is a problem.

The word "new" appears in all three headlines. It may not help us distinguish one headline from another.

We want to give more importance to words that are useful for distinguishing headlines.

That is where TF-IDF comes in.

### What does TF-IDF mean?

TF-IDF stands for:

- TF — Term Frequency: How often does a word appear in a particular document?
- IDF — Inverse Document Frequency: How uncommon is that word across all documents?

A document here simply means one headline.

Simplest definition: TF-IDF gives numerical importance scores to words based on how often they appear in one headline and how uncommon they are across the whole collection.

Let's use a simple analogy.

Imagine a classroom of 30 students.

Every student uses the word "school". It is a very common word.

But only one student uses the word "astronaut".

If you want to identify which student is talking about space, the word "astronaut" is more useful than "school".

TF-IDF tries to give more importance to words that are more distinctive.

### Why are we using TF-IDF?

Because our AI models need numbers, not raw sentences.

Our process becomes:

Headline

"Scientists discover a new planet"

TF-IDF

Words become numerical scores

AI model

Uses those numbers to make a prediction

TF-IDF is not itself the fake-news detector. It is a tool that prepares the text for the detector.

# 7. Training data and testing data — Why do we divide the dataset?

Imagine you are preparing for a mathematics exam.

Your teacher gives you 100 practice questions with answers.

You study 80 questions.

Then your teacher gives you 20 new questions that you have never seen before.

Why?

Because if you only answer questions you memorised, the teacher cannot know whether you actually learned mathematics.

The same idea applies to our AI model.

We divide our labelled dataset into two parts:

## Example: 1,000 labelled headlines

Training data

### 800

The model studies these examples and learns patterns.

Testing data

### 200

The model is tested on these examples that it did not use for training.

This is called a train/test split.

Simplest definition: A train/test split divides the data into one part for learning and another part for checking how well the model learned.

### Why do we need it?

Suppose our model gets 100% accuracy on the headlines it studied.

That does not mean it will correctly predict new headlines.

It might simply have memorised the examples.

Testing with unseen data helps us check whether it can recognise patterns beyond the examples it studied.

### What is unseen data?

Unseen data means examples that were not used to train the model.

It does not mean the computer has never encountered any similar words. It means those particular test examples were kept separate from training.

### One important rule: No cheating!

Imagine a student secretly reads the answer key before the examination.

The result will look excellent, but it is not a fair test.

In machine learning, this problem is called data leakage.

For example, if duplicate headlines appear in both training and testing sets, the model may appear more successful than it really is.

We must also fit TF-IDF using training data only, then use that already-fitted TF-IDF transformation on the test data. Otherwise, information from the test set can leak into the training process.

# 12. Evaluation metrics — How do we measure the model's performance?

Imagine you have two students who take a test with 100 questions.

You don't just want to hear "I did well!"

You want to see their actual marks and mistakes.

Our AI models also need a proper examination.

There are four important measurements.

## A. Accuracy

Simplest definition: Accuracy tells us what percentage of all predictions were correct.

Imagine the model examines 100 headlines:

- 90 predictions are correct.
- 10 predictions are incorrect.

Its accuracy is 90%.

\\[ Accuracy=\frac{\text{Correct predictions}}{\text{Total predictions}} \\]

Why do we need it?

It gives us a simple overall picture of how often the model gets the answer right.

But accuracy alone can be misleading.

Imagine 100 headlines:

- 90 are real.
- 10 are fake.

A model that always predicts Real gets 90% accuracy!

But it detects absolutely none of the fake headlines.

That is why we need more measurements.

## B. Precision

Imagine the model points to 20 headlines and says:

"These 20 headlines are fake."

After checking the correct answers, we discover:

- 15 really are fake.
- 5 are actually real.

Precision tells us how many of the headlines predicted as fake were actually fake.

\\[ Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} \\]

Here, for our example:

\\[ Precision=\frac{15}{20}=75\\% \\]

Simplest definition: Precision tells us how often the model is correct when it predicts a particular category.

For our project, we will define Fake as the positive category when calculating fake-class precision.

## C. Recall

Now imagine there are 30 actually fake headlines in the examination.

Our model correctly detects 15 of them.

But it misses the other 15.

Recall tells us how many of the actual fake headlines the model successfully found.

\\[ Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} \\]

In this example:

\\[ Recall=\frac{15}{30}=50\\% \\]

Simplest definition: Recall tells us how many of the actual examples in a category the model successfully detects.

Why do we need it?

A model might have high precision but miss many fake headlines. Recall helps us see that problem.

## D. F1-score

Imagine one model has:

- High precision.
- Low recall.

Another model has:

- Low precision.
- High recall.

Which one is better?

We need a measurement that considers both.

That is the F1-score.

Simplest definition: F1-score combines precision and recall into one measurement.

\\[ F1=2\times\frac{Precision\times Recall}{Precision+Recall} \\]

It is useful when we want to understand the balance between finding fake headlines and avoiding incorrect fake predictions.

Remember the difference

| Metric    | The question it answers                             |
| --------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Accuracy  | How many predictions were correct overall?          |
| Precision | When the model says Fake, how often is it right?    |
| Recall    | Of all actual Fake headlines, how many did it find? |
| F1-score  | How well does it balance precision and recall?      |

These are not four different AI models. They are four different ways to measure the performance of our models.

### What is clustering?

Simplest definition: Clustering means automatically grouping similar items together.

### What is K-means?

K-means is an algorithm that divides data into a chosen number of groups, called clusters, based on similarity.

The letter K represents the number of groups we want.

For example:

- K = 2 means two groups.
- K = 3 means three groups.
- K = 4 means four groups.

### Let's apply it to our headlines

Imagine we have these six headlines:

| Headline                                  | Possible topic |
| ----------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| Government announces new education policy | Education      |
| Schools introduce new examination system  | Education      |
| Cricket team wins international match     | Sports         |
| Pakistan wins cricket tournament          | Sports         |
| New smartphone released                   | Technology     |
| Company launches new computer             | Technology     |

We ask K-means to create three groups.

It may discover:

Cluster 1

Education-related headlines

- Government announces new education policy
- Schools introduce new examination system

Cluster 2

Sports-related headlines

- Cricket team wins international match
- Pakistan wins cricket tournament

Cluster 3

Technology-related headlines

- New smartphone released
- Company launches new computer

These are illustrative groups. Actual K-means clusters are not guaranteed to be so clean or meaningful.

### What does "unlabelled headlines" mean?

Remember our labelled dataset?

| Headline                    | Label |
| --------------------------- | ----- |
| Government announces policy | Real  |
| Aliens visit Karachi        | Fake  |

For supervised learning, we use the labels Real and Fake.

But for clustering, we can take headlines without their Real/Fake labels and ask the algorithm to group them according to their word patterns.

Unlabelled means that the correct category is not provided to the algorithm for that task.

### Why do we need K-means?

Because our project should do more than predict Real or Fake.

It should also explore what topics appear in the collection of headlines.

For example:

- What groups of topics are present?
- Which words are common in each group?
- Do similar headlines appear together?

### How does K-means work?

Imagine you want to organise 100 children into three groups based on their similarities.

K-means follows a simple repeating process:

1. Choose three starting group centres.
2. Assign each headline to its nearest centre.
3. Calculate a better centre for each group.
4. Assign the headlines again.
5. Repeat until the groups stop changing much.

These group centres are called centroids.

Simplest definition: A centroid is the centre point representing a cluster.

### Why do we use TF-IDF again?

Because K-means also needs numerical information.

It cannot directly calculate the distance between two English sentences in the way it calculates distances between numbers.

So:

- TF-IDF turns headlines into numerical features.
- K-means uses those features to group similar headlines.

### Very important distinction

Our two classification models answer:

"Is this headline likely Real or Fake?"

K-means answers:

"Which other headlines have similar word patterns?"

K-means does not decide whether a headline is real or fake.

It is a separate, unsupervised learning task.

# 19. Environment classification — What kind of world does our AI work in?

This is another requirement from your AI course.

Imagine different robots working in different places.

- One robot works in a changing traffic environment.
- Another works in a fixed classroom.
- One robot works alone.
- Another works with other robots.

We describe these environments using certain properties.

For our headline detector, we can classify its prediction environment like this:

| Property      | Simple meaning                                          | Our project                                                        |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Observable    | Can the system see the information it needs?            | Fully observable for the submitted headline: its text is available |
| Deterministic | Does the same action produce the same result?           | Deterministic during prediction with a fixed model and settings    |
| Static        | Does the situation change while the system is deciding? | Static: the submitted headline does not change during prediction   |
| Discrete      | Are the possible outcomes separate categories?          | Discrete: Likely Real or Likely Fake                               |
| Single-agent  | Is one intelligent agent making the decision?           | Single-agent: our classifier works without competing agents        |

### Let's understand these words in ordinary language.

Fully observable: Imagine you are solving a puzzle and can see every piece. You have all the information available for that task.

In our case, the model receives the complete headline text.

However, this does not mean it knows the actual truth behind the headline. It only sees the information provided to it.

Deterministic: Imagine pressing a calculator's `2 + 2` button. With the same settings, you get the same answer.

Similarly, a fixed trained classifier given the same headline should produce the same prediction.

Static: Imagine a photograph. It does not change while you look at it.

Our submitted headline remains unchanged while the classifier processes it.

Discrete: Imagine a traffic light. Its possible states are separate: Red, Yellow and Green.

Our prediction has two separate categories: Real and Fake.

Single-agent: Imagine one student solving an individual examination rather than playing a game against another student.

Our classifier makes its prediction without competing against another intelligent agent.

These classifications describe the prediction task, not the entire internet or the truthfulness of every news story.

# 21. Reproducible experiments — Why do we need a fixed random seed?

Imagine you throw a dice today and get 4.

Tomorrow you throw it again and get 2.

The result changes because dice rolls involve randomness.

Some machine-learning operations also involve random choices.

For example:

- Which examples go into the training set.
- Which examples go into the testing set.
- Where K-means initially places its cluster centres.

If we run the experiment again, we may get different results.

That makes comparisons difficult.

### What is a random seed?

A random seed is like setting a starting point for a random-number generator.

If we use the same seed and the same settings, we can reproduce many random operations.

Simplest definition: A random seed helps us repeat an experiment with the same random starting conditions.

For example:

```
random_state=42
```

The number 42 is just an example. We can choose another fixed number.

### Why do we need it?

Imagine we run our project today:

- Naive Bayes accuracy: 85%.
- Logistic Regression accuracy: 90%.

Tomorrow we run it again with a different random split:

- Naive Bayes accuracy: 82%.
- Logistic Regression accuracy: 87%.

Which results should we put in our report?

Using fixed random seeds makes our experiment more consistent and allows us to reproduce the reported results.

For our project, we should set the random state wherever the relevant library supports it, including the data split and K-means initialisation. We should also seed Python's `random` module as the general guidelines explicitly request.

Important: A seed does not guarantee identical results across every computer, software version or hardware configuration. We should also record our software versions and experiment settings.

# 26. What are limitations and ethical considerations?

Imagine you build a machine that predicts whether fruit is ripe.

But your machine learned from only 100 apples.

Would you confidently use it to judge every fruit in the world?

No!

It may work well for apples but badly for mangoes.

Our fake-news detector has similar limitations.

### Limitations

A limitation is something our system cannot do perfectly or reliably.

For example:

1. Dataset limitation: If our dataset contains poor-quality labels, the model may learn incorrect patterns.
2. Bias: If most examples come from certain sources, the model may perform poorly on headlines from other sources.
3. Changing news: Language and news topics change over time.
4. Misleading headlines: A headline may sound unusual but still describe a real event.
5. Limited understanding: Our models examine text patterns. They do not independently investigate the event or verify its facts.

### Ethical considerations

Ethics means thinking about whether our system is being used responsibly and fairly.

Imagine our detector labels a genuine news headline as fake.

Someone shares that prediction and damages the reputation of a news organisation.

That could be harmful.

So we must:

- Avoid claiming that our model is a perfect fact-checker.
- Display predictions as likely real or likely fake.
- Explain that predictions are based on learned dataset patterns.
- Avoid presenting model accuracy as proof of truth.
- Discuss possible bias and incorrect predictions.

The main lesson: Our AI helps analyse headlines. It does not replace human fact-checking.
